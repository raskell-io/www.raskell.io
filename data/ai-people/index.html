People of the AI labs, 1955 to 2026

Who worked where in AI and when, how they were tied to each other, and which idea led to which product, under the paradigms of their time, from two books.

This is the data behind the two figures in my note Who contributed to AI: the people who carried the idea of a learning machine from the 1950s to the labs of today, where each of them worked and when, and how they were tied to one another. I compiled it while reading Karen Hao’s Empire of AI and Sebastian Mallaby’s The Infinity Machine back to back, and I am publishing it on its own so that you can draw something else from it.

What a record is

The file is one JSON object. The core of it is 31 people. Each person has an id, a name, a one-line summary, a url to read more, and a list of stints. A stint is one role at one organisation: org, role, start, end (null while it lasts), place, and left, a sentence on why it ended. There are 126 stints in all.

Around the people sit more parts:

  • orgs: 101 labs, companies, universities and funders, keyed by id, each with a name, a kind, the year or month it was founded, its place, and a short note.
  • places: 22 places, from the Bay Area to London and Toronto, so that a stint can be drawn where it happened.
  • links: 67 ties between two people, with a kind (mentor, ally, rival, cofounders, and a few rarer ones), a date, and a note.
  • moments: 258 ideas, products and events, each with an id, a date, a title, a detail, and the people and organisations it involves. Ideas and products also carry a track, the field they belong to, and long titles a short label for charts.
  • tracks: the 10 fields that ideas and products are sorted into, from games and search to assistants and agents, in the order the figure draws them.
  • paradigms: 10 eras of AI research, from symbolic AI to agents, each with a start, an end (null while it lasts) and a summary. They overlap, because a new way of working rarely ends the old one at once.
  • lineage: 79 links that say one idea or product built on another: from and to are moment ids, and a note says how.

Dates are YYYY, YYYY-MM or YYYY-MM-DD, as precise as the source allows and no more. The about object at the top of the file repeats the title and the licence and describes the main fields, so the file still makes sense when it travels without this page. range says which years the figure draws, and where its compressed early decades end.

Where each fact comes from

Every stint, tie, moment, paradigm and lineage link carries src: hao, mallaby, web, or more than one of them. Facts from a book carry ref, the chapter they come from, so that you can check them against your own copy. Facts I took from outside the books were checked on the web, and each of them carries the url I checked it against.

How I compiled it

I read both books in full and wrote every fact down in my own words, with the chapter it comes from. The summaries, roles, reasons for leaving and moment details are my sentences, not the authors’. Before publishing I ran a check against the text of both books, and no run of eight words in this file is copied from either of them.

The lineage links are the ones a source states or plainly describes, such as GPT-3 being fine-tuned into Codex, or Bard running on LaMDA. They are not a full family tree of the field: an idea with no link here may still have shaped many others.

What it does not cover

The cast is the one the two books follow, so it leans towards OpenAI, DeepMind and the people around them. Many who did important work in the field are missing because neither book is about them, and I did not add people the books do not discuss. The ties are the ones the books describe; two people without a tie here may still have known each other well. A left field is my summary of what the source says, not a verdict on what happened.

Portraits

Twenty of the people have a portrait. The portraits come from Wikimedia Commons under their own licences; each person’s photo_credit names the photographer, the licence and the Commons page. They are not covered by the licence of this dataset; reuse them under their own terms.

Changes

  • 2 November 2026: added the paradigms, the tracks and the lineage, and seven products and ideas they needed (GitHub Copilot, DALL-E 2, Constitutional AI, Bard, Bing chat, Llama, DeepSeek R1). Merged fourteen records that described the same thing twice. Corrected two: Ilya Sutskever is no longer listed on the Transformer paper, which he did not write, and the Vision Transformer is now Google Brain’s rather than tied to Hassabis and Jumper. Moments now carry an id.
  • 1 November 2026: first published.
