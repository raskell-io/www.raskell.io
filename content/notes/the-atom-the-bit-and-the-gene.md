In the introduction to *The Code Breaker*, Walter Isaacson divides modern times into three revolutions, each built on one of what he calls the fundamental kernels of our existence: "the atom, the bit, and the gene."

- **The atom.** The first half of the twentieth century belonged to physics. It starts with Einstein's papers of 1905 and runs, within fifty years, to nuclear power, the transistor, lasers, radar, and spaceflight.
- **The bit.** The second half belonged to information: the idea that any information can be written as binary digits and any logic built from switches. It gave us the microchip, the computer, and the internet.
- **The gene.** The third, Isaacson argues, is starting now: a revolution in the life sciences, in which children who learn to write code for computers will be joined by those who learn to read and write the genetic code.

This is my own conviction too, with two adjustments. The first is where AI and robotics belong. I do not see them as a fourth revolution. They are the information revolution reaching its full extent: the bit learning to see, to speak, and to move things in the world. That is a large part of why it feels so sudden now, but it is the end of the second wave, not the start of a new one.

The second is how early we are in the third. CRISPR, the subject of Isaacson's book, is often told as the moment the gene revolution arrived. I think it is closer to the first transistor than to the internet. We can now cut and edit a sequence, but we still understand only a small part of what most of it does, how cells read it, and how its parts work together. Physics needed about fifty years to get from Einstein's papers to the power plant, and computing about as long to get from the first transistor to the web. If the life sciences follow the same arc, the big revolutions are still ahead of us: in medicine, in ageing, in food, in materials, and in what we understand about life itself.

The two revolutions also feed each other. The bit is now one of the main tools of the gene: DeepMind's AlphaFold, which I wrote about in my review of [*The Infinity Machine*](@/articles/win-everything-control-no-one.md), predicted the shapes of proteins that laboratories had spent decades trying to determine. And the gene keeps showing up in computing, from neurons in a dish that learn to play a game to the [brain emulation](@/articles/brain-emulation-as-an-infrastructure-problem.md) projects that treat a nervous system as an infrastructure problem. The meeting point of the bit and the gene is where I expect most of the surprises.
