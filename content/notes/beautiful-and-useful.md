Two themes run through the introduction of Walter Isaacson's *The Code Breaker*. One comes from Jennifer Doudna herself. Talking about CRISPR, a defence that bacteria use against viruses and that people now use as a tool, she says: "Nature is beautiful that way." Isaacson asks the reader to remember the phrase, because the book keeps returning to it. Part Two opens with Henri Poincaré saying much the same thing: the scientist studies nature not because it is useful but because it is beautiful.

The other theme is Isaacson's own. Above all, he writes, he wants to convey the importance of basic science, "curiosity-driven rather than application-oriented", because research into the wonders of nature plants the seeds of later inventions, sometimes in ways nobody could have predicted. Surface physics led to the transistor. A strange pattern in bacterial DNA led to gene editing.

I am on the other side of that line by temperament. I am pragmatic, and I want to build things that solve problems, so most of my curiosity goes into the second kind of work: how to make something run, cheaper, safer, for more people. But I see the value of the first kind, and I think it is the nobler of the two. Someone who studies a pattern in bacterial DNA for years with no use in sight, because it is beautiful and unexplained, is doing something I mostly do not do, and the rest of us live off it later.

What I like is that Isaacson does not leave the split clean. In chapter 12, "The Yogurt Makers", he takes apart the linear model of innovation, the idea from Vannevar Bush's 1945 report that pure science comes first and products follow. The transistor came out of Bell Labs, where theorists sat next to engineers who knew how to amplify a phone signal. And CRISPR's function was confirmed in part by food scientists at Danisco, who studied it because viruses kept destroying the bacteria in their yogurt and cheese cultures. Isaacson quotes Matt Ridley's point that invention is just as often the parent of science: the steam engine came before thermodynamics, and powered flight before most of aerodynamics.

That is the version I believe. Curiosity and usefulness are not two kinds of people. They are two directions of the same loop, and the loop turns fastest where both sit in one room: a lab next to a factory, a researcher next to a customer with a problem. It is also why I argued that progress is carried by people who have to make things work ([who carries progress](@/notes/who-carries-progress.md)), while admitting that much of what they build stands on research nobody paid them to do.
