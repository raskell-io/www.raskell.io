Chapter 3 of Nassim Nicholas Taleb's *Skin in the Game*, "How to Legally Own Another Person", asks why companies keep employees at all when contractors are cheaper and more flexible. His example is a small airline whose contract pilot, Bob, gets a better offer the morning of a full Oktoberfest flight and simply does not show up. A contractor, Taleb writes, fears mostly the law. *"But employees have a reputation to protect. And they can be fired."* So *"employees exist because they have significant skin in the game,"* and what the firm is really paying for is this: *"You are buying dependability."*

Read that in 2026 and the AI agent looks like the perfect employee. It is always there, never tired, never poached by a competitor on the morning of a flight, and costs almost nothing when there is no work. It fails Taleb's test in exactly one place. It has no reputation, no career, and nothing to lose. You cannot fire it in any sense that it would notice.

That does not make agents undependable; it moves the dependability somewhere else. In a firm of people, the risk of a bad decision is spread along the chain: the person who made it, their manager, the team. In a firm of agents, there is nobody in the chain who can be ashamed, sued, or let go. All of the reputation that used to be shared by a team now sits with whoever runs the agents. One person with a thousand agents does not have a thousand employees with skin in the game. They have one, themselves.

That is why I think ownership is what is left when code gets cheap, as I argued in my [review of the book](@/articles/review-skin-in-the-game.md). It is also why I design agent platforms so that every agent has a human sponsor whose name is on its actions. The agent can do the work. Only a person can be the one you trust to answer for it.
