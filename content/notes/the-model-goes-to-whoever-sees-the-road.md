For most of history, the company with the best data about a risk was the insurer that covered it: the claims, the actuaries, the tables. That is no longer guaranteed. A modern car knows how hard you brake, how fast you corner and what time of night you drive, and Tesla already prices insurance in parts of the United States from a safety score computed from the car's own data. George Hotz has floated the idea that comma.ai's long-term business could be insurance for the same reason: a company whose devices see millions of miles of driving knows who drives well.

The general rule is simple. Whoever has the best model of a risk can offer the best price for it, and the best model goes to whoever sees the world it describes. Phones see how people move, watches see how they sleep, buildings increasingly see their own leaks. Each of those sensors is an underwriting model held by someone who is not an insurer. The incumbents who think of themselves as data companies will notice in time; the ones who do not will end up as balance sheets renting someone else's model, as I argued in [The Original Data Company](/articles/the-original-data-company/).
