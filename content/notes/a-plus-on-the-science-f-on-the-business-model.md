In August 2010 the three DeepMind founders went to the Singularity Summit in San Francisco because they were out of options. No investor who had backed Hassabis's games studio would touch a company whose product was general intelligence, and the only capital so far was a professor's hundred thousand pounds and a religious financier who made founders pitch to his teenage sons. Thiel was at his own party near the Golden Gate, Eliezer Yudkowsky walked them over, and Hassabis, who had prepared for the moment for a year, opened not with a pitch but with chess: the bishop and the knight carry the same value and do entirely different things. It got him a meeting the next day.

Mallaby reports Thiel's conclusion from that meeting as a grade: A-plus on the science, somewhere around F on the business model. He invested anyway, 2.3 million dollars that December for a bit less than half the company, and the reason is the part worth keeping. Thiel's distinction is between the mercenary founder, who wants a company and goes looking for an idea, and the missionary, who is compelled by a problem and starts a company because it is the only way to work on it. Missionaries do not quit; they work for nothing and keep obsessing. His line was that most people are not entrepreneurs in the abstract, but a few have one great company in them, and this was Hassabis's.

I think about that grade when I look at the [ventures on this site](/invest/), and at the [citizen entrepreneurs](/notes/citizen-entrepreneurs/) idea. The tools now make the business-model half cheap to try. They do nothing for the science half or for the missionary half, and those were the ones the grade was really about.
