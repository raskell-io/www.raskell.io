The textbook story of money is that people bartered, found it awkward, and invented coins. David Graeber spent a large part of *Debt* showing that the order is the other way round: "We did not begin with barter, discover money, and then eventually develop credit systems. It happened precisely the other way around. What we now call virtual money came first." Mesopotamian temples kept accounts of who owed what to whom thousands of years before the first coins appeared, between roughly 600 and 500 BC.

I like the idea because it removes a misunderstanding about what money is. Coins and banknotes are the unusual, recent form. The normal form is a record: an entry in a ledger that says one party owes another, which is worth exactly as much as people believe the record will be honoured. That is why the old word for it, credit, from *credere*, to believe, is still the most precise one, and why the institutions that keep such records are, as I argued in [The Original Data Company](/articles/the-original-data-company/), in the business of formalising trust.
