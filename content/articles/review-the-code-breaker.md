## Reading notes

### Introduction, "Into the Breach"

**p. xvi. The twins, and Adam, Eve, and Prometheus.** Context: March 2020, Doudna rallies Berkeley scientists against
the coronavirus. Isaacson raises inheritable edits that could make our descendants less vulnerable to viruses;
Doudna calls that science fiction. Isaacson answers that parts of it have already happened: in November 2018 a
young Chinese scientist (He Jiankui, told in Part Six) used CRISPR on embryos to remove the gene for a receptor HIV
uses, and twin girls were born, "the world's first 'designer babies.'"

The next paragraph frames the moment as a threshold: humanity has taken control of its own genetic future, a sense
of entering a new age, likened to Adam and Eve biting into the apple and Prometheus taking fire from the gods.

- What I like: the religious and mythological imagery, the fall and the stolen fire, says more about the moment
  than the deep-time framing Isaacson puts beside it. Use the images, not the time scale.
- The book itself leans on these images. Part One opens with Genesis 2:8 to 9 (Eden, the tree of life and the tree
  of the knowledge of good and evil). Prometheus returns three times: Aeschylus's *Prometheus Bound* (Part Four),
  the "winged hound of Zeus" in the epigraph of Part Five, and Shelley's *Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus*
  (Part Six). Part Seven opens with James Watson: "If scientists don't play God, who will?" A thread for the review.
- The two images point in different directions. Eden is knowledge taken against a command, and the cost is exile.
  Prometheus is a gift taken for people's sake, and the cost is borne by the giver. Which one fits gene editing,
  and who decides, is the question of Part Seven ("Who Should Decide?").
- Possible note. Link to [something closer to a religion](@/notes/something-closer-to-a-religion.md) and the
  Rickover note on a person acting as if the fate of the world depended on them.

**Same chapter, for later.** Isaacson's frame for the book: three revolutions of modern times built on "the atom,
the bit, and the gene". Physics in the first half of the twentieth century, information in the second, and now
life science. A bridge to the AI reviews: Hassabis's AlphaFold sits exactly where the bit meets the gene.

**p. xviii. "Nature is beautiful."** Doudna's own line, about CRISPR being an old bacterial defence that people now
use as a tool; Isaacson tells the reader to remember the phrase as a theme of the book. It rhymes with the Part Two
epigraph from Poincaré (the scientist studies nature because it is beautiful, not because it is useful).

**p. xix. Basic science.** Isaacson's frame, not Doudna's: above all he wants to convey the importance of basic
science, curiosity-driven rather than application-oriented, which plants the seeds of later inventions.

- My position: I am pragmatic and build solutions, so my curiosity goes mostly to the applied side; I see the value
  of the basic side and think it is the nobler cause.
- Isaacson complicates his own split in chapter 12, "The Yogurt Makers": the linear model (Vannevar Bush, 1945)
  against Bell Labs and the Danisco food scientists who helped confirm what CRISPR does; Matt Ridley on invention
  as the parent of science. Developed in the draft note `content/notes/beautiful-and-useful.md`.
- The three revolutions on p. xvii are in the draft note `content/notes/the-atom-the-bit-and-the-gene.md`.
